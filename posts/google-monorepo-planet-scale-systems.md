Today, millions of people hit "download" on a 200 GB video game, and the internet did not fall over.

That is not luck. It is a stack of unglamorous engineering decisions: DNS tricks that steer each player to a nearby server, several CDNs sharing the load, and a preload window that spread the traffic across days. Network researchers at RIPE NCC even measured it. They found players in different cities being routed to different download sites, with AWS, Fastly and Akamai all carrying a share of the weight.

Now hold that thought, because for one company, that kind of problem is not a launch-day event. It is Tuesday.

This article is about what happens when software gets so big that every normal tool breaks. We will walk through a single code repository that weighs 86 terabytes, a database that keeps time with atomic clocks, and a cluster manager that became the ancestor of Kubernetes. Along the way, we will arrive at an uncomfortable idea that explains why some engineers get paid crores and others do not:

**Writing code was never the hard part.**

---

## The uncomfortable truth about "programming"

If you learned to code recently, you probably learned it as a loop: think of a feature, type it, run it, fix it. That loop is real, and it is how every engineer starts.

But inside Google, there is a saying that reframes the whole job. The people who wrote the book on how Google builds software put it this way: "Software engineering is programming integrated over time."

Read that again slowly, because it is the key to this entire article.

Programming is producing a piece of code that works today. Software engineering is keeping that code correct, changeable and understandable for ten or twenty years, while hundreds of other teams depend on it and the world around it keeps changing. The authors list three things that separate the two: **time**, **scale**, and **trade-offs**. Time means your code will outlive your assumptions. Scale means a decision that is fine for ten engineers is a disaster for ten thousand. And trade-offs means nothing is free, so you are always choosing which pain to accept.

Here is where it gets spicy. In April 2026, Google's CEO said that about 75 percent of all new code at Google is now generated by AI and then approved by engineers. A year and a half earlier, in late 2024, the figure was a bit over a quarter, and by late 2025 it was around half. (To be precise, that claim is about *new* code, not the whole codebase.) He also described a complex code migration that finished about six times faster when engineers and AI agents worked together.

Sit with that for a second. At one of the most elite engineering organizations on Earth, typing the code is increasingly the cheap step.

So what is still expensive? Everything the rest of this article is about:

- Deciding *what* should exist, and what should not.
- Making a change that touches a million files without breaking a single one.
- Keeping a database correct while it spans the planet.
- Noticing, at 3 a.m., which of your 40 dependencies is quietly lying to you.

The code is the easy part. The system is the job.

## Meet the monster: Google's monorepo

Most of us store code in many small repositories: one per service, one per library, maybe dozens per team. Google went the other way. For the overwhelming majority of its software, there is **one repository**. One tree. One history. One "source of truth" that tens of thousands of engineers read and write every single day.

The numbers below come from the 2016 Communications of the ACM paper by Rachel Potvin and Josh Levenberg, describing the state of things in January 2015. They are a decade old, and the real figures today are almost certainly bigger, but they still make the point.

| Metric | Value (2015 data) |
|---|---|
| Total data stored | about 86 TB |
| Unique source files | about 9 million |
| Lines of code | about 2 billion |
| Total files (including generated, config, release branches) | about 1 billion |
| Commits in history | about 35 million, across 18 years |
| Commits per day | around 40,000 (about 16,000 by humans, about 24,000 by automation) |
| Engineers with access | about 25,000 |
| Read traffic | billions of file reads a day, around 800,000 queries per second at peak |
| Lines changed per week (2014) | roughly 15 million, across about 250,000 files |

For comparison, the Linux kernel, one of the biggest open-source projects in existence, was around 15 million lines in 40,000 files. Google's repository was roughly two orders of magnitude bigger.

Let me make that physical, because "2 billion" slides right off the brain:

- If you read one line of code per second, nonstop, no sleep, it would take you about **63 years** to read the whole thing.
- Printed at 50 lines per page, the stack would be around **4 kilometres tall**.
- And here is the detail that should make you look twice: most of those daily commits are not typed by humans. **Roughly three out of five are made by automated systems**: formatters, code generators, dependency updaters, and large-scale cleanup bots. The repository is partly maintained by robots.

Also worth noticing: it is not just a pile of files. It is a *service*. Which brings us to the first piece of custom machinery.

## "Just use Git" (and why it was never going to work)

For over ten years, Google ran its central codebase on a single Perforce server, with a lot of proprietary caching bolted on to keep it alive. That bought time. But as the company and its codebase kept growing, the approach ran out of road.

So Google built its own version-control system, called **Piper**. Around 2012, the central repository moved onto it.

Here is the part most people miss. Git, as most of us use it, stores a repository as data that every developer *clones* onto their machine. You cannot clone 86 TB onto a laptop. So Piper is not a program you run locally. It is a **distributed service** that holds the canonical history, built on top of Google's own infrastructure: first Bigtable, later Spanner, replicated across roughly ten data centres and kept consistent with the Paxos consensus algorithm.

Think about what that means. Google's *source code* lives in a globally replicated, strongly consistent database, the same family of technology that runs its ads and its mail. The repository is logically centralized, but physically scattered across the planet.

### CitC: the trick that makes it feel local

If you cannot clone the repo, how do you work on it? Through a system called **CitC**, short for "Clients in the Cloud".

CitC presents the entire codebase to you as a normal-looking filesystem, backed by cloud storage. You can browse and edit any file anywhere in the tree with ordinary tools, but only the files you have actually *modified* are stored in your workspace. The paper reports that an average workspace holds fewer than ten files, even though it appears to contain everything.

```text
   Your workstation
         |
         |   looks like the whole company's code, right there
         v
   +--------------+
   |     CitC     |   stores only your edits (often under ten files)
   +------+-------+
          |   everything else falls through to...
          v
   +--------------+
   |    Piper     |   one logical tree, one history
   +------+-------+
          |
          v
   +--------------+
   |   Spanner    |   Paxos-replicated across about ten data centres
   +--------------+
```

It is a beautiful trick: the illusion of owning a copy of everything, with the cost of owning almost nothing.

## The tooling iceberg

Here is the thing nobody tells you about monorepos. The repo itself is the *easy* part (relatively). The reason it does not collapse under its own weight is the pile of tools around it. Google's papers and engineering writing describe a whole ecosystem:

| Tool | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Piper** | The version-control service that holds the single source of truth |
| **CitC** | The cloud-backed workspace that makes the giant tree feel local |
| **Blaze (Bazel)** | The build system: hermetic, incremental, cached builds driven by a declared dependency graph |
| **TAP** | The test automation platform that runs tests on affected targets, with auto-rollback for bad changes |
| **Critique** | Code review; nothing is committed without review |
| **Tricorder** | Static analysis that runs on changes before they land |
| **Code Search** | Browsing, navigating and understanding a codebase too big to hold in your head |
| **OWNERS files** | A tree of owners who must approve changes to their part of the code |
| **Rosie** | Splits huge cross-company changes into smaller, reviewable pieces and shepherds them through |

Notice how each one exists because a *specific* thing broke at scale. The build system has to be hermetic and cached, or nobody could ever wait for a build. Testing has to be selective and automated, or you cannot know what your change broke. Code search has to be a product in its own right, or nobody can find anything.

The monorepo is not a repository choice. It is a commitment to build a whole company-sized developer-productivity platform around it.

## One trunk, no branches, and the superpower of atomic change

Another thing that looks insane from the outside: Google engineers work at **head**. Everybody commits to the same trunk. Long-lived feature branches are rare. Release branches exist, but mostly for stable releases that receive cherry-picked fixes.

Why on earth would you do this? Because of what it unlocks.

### Imagine the alternative

Suppose a widely used library has a function with a terrible name, and you want to fix it. In a world of many repositories, you would:

1. Release a new version of the library with the new name.
2. Wait for hundreds of teams to upgrade, each on their own schedule.
3. Support both versions in the meantime.
4. Watch a few teams never upgrade at all.

And then there is the classic nightmare called the *diamond dependency*:

```text
   Your app --> Library A --> JSON library v1
   Your app --> Library B --> JSON library v2     (which one do you get?)
```

In a monorepo, that question mostly disappears: there is one version of everything, and it is whatever is at head. When the library changes, *everything that uses it changes in the same commit*. The authors of the ACM paper list the benefits plainly: a single source of truth, extensive code sharing, simpler dependency management, atomic changes, large-scale refactoring, and flexible team boundaries.

So the fix for that terrible function name is: change the library and every caller, in one atomic operation, with the test system verifying that nothing broke. For a change that touches thousands of files, a tool like Rosie slices it into per-owner chunks, runs the tests, and requests the reviews. The messy part, getting all the humans to agree, becomes a pipeline instead of a negotiation.

Here is a rough picture of what a change goes through:

```text
write code
   -> presubmit checks (tests + static analysis)
   -> code review in Critique (+ approval from the owners of the touched directories)
   -> submit to head
   -> TAP re-tests everything that depends on it
   -> automatic rollback if something breaks
```

### The honest part: it is not free

Before anyone rushes to merge all their repos into one, a warning. The same sources that praise this model are upfront about the costs: it demands heavy investment in custom tooling, infrastructure for builds and tests, and constant discipline about code health. Google also made some policy choices to keep the tree sane, like making APIs private by default so teams must explicitly declare what others may depend on.

And even the Google engineers who wrote the engineering book are careful to say their scale and resources are wholly unlike most organizations, so they do not claim this is "the one true way."

The lesson is not "use a monorepo." The lesson is *understand the problem so deeply that you are willing to build a company-sized solution for it.*

## When the world's databases are not big enough

Now zoom out from code to data, because the same story repeats at every layer.

In the early 2000s, Google published a sequence of papers that quietly reshaped the industry: the Google File System, MapReduce, Bigtable. Each one existed because the off-the-shelf option assumed a ceiling that Google had already blown through. Then, in 2012, came the one that still makes database people go quiet: **Spanner**.

### The impossible-sounding promise

Distributed-systems folk have long repeated a rule of thumb: at global scale, you must trade away either strong consistency or scalability. Pick one.

Spanner's pitch was: we would like both, please.

The paper describes it as a scalable, multi-version, globally distributed, synchronously replicated database, and the first system to offer externally consistent distributed transactions at global scale. It was designed to scale to millions of machines across hundreds of data centres, holding trillions of rows. And it supports SQL and full transactions.

How? By attacking the one thing everyone else treats as unfixable: **time**.

### The clock problem

In a distributed system, every machine has its own clock, and clocks drift. If server A in Singapore says it is 10:00:00.000 and server B in London says 09:59:59.998, which one is right? If you cannot agree on the order of events, you cannot agree on what the data looks like. Two transactions might each think they went first.

The usual answer is to avoid trusting clocks at all. Spanner's answer was audacious: **make the clocks trustworthy.**

Google built an API called **TrueTime**. Instead of returning a single timestamp, it returns an *interval*: "the true time is somewhere between *earliest* and *latest*." And it keeps that interval tiny, in the single-digit milliseconds, by putting real hardware into its data centres: GPS receivers (with dedicated antennas) on most time-master machines, and atomic clocks on the rest. The paper cheerfully calls those backup machines "Armageddon masters."

Then comes the clever part. When Spanner commits a transaction, it picks a timestamp, and then **deliberately waits out the uncertainty** (a few milliseconds) before declaring the commit done. That brief pause guarantees that if one transaction finishes before another starts, the first one's timestamp is smaller. The database is paying a few milliseconds of latency to buy a global order of events.

Atomic clocks, GPS satellites, and a mandatory little nap before every commit. That is the kind of idea you can only have when the ordinary solution has already failed you.

### How big has it gotten?

Google keeps publishing numbers, and they keep climbing. A few data points from Google and from executives speaking to the press:

| When | Peak queries per second | Data under management |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 2023 | about 3 billion | more than 12 exabytes |
| Jan 2026 (describing 2025) | over 6 billion | more than 17 exabytes |
| Jun 2026 | about 7.5 billion | about 23 exabytes |

(The 2023 figure came with a marketing comparison to a competitor's database, so treat the framing with salt. The 2026 figures are spoken remarks by a Google database executive, not a formal benchmark.)

Make those real:

- **7.5 billion queries per second** is roughly the number of human beings on Earth, *every second*.
- **23 exabytes** is about 23 billion gigabytes.
- Google's own Bigtable was described in the same interview as handling around 7 billion queries per second, with double-digit exabytes of its own.

And this is just two systems out of a very large garden. Remember that Spanner is also, according to the descriptions above, where Piper keeps the monorepo's history. The codebase and the data it serves run on the same planet-scale foundation.

## The machine that runs the machines

Code and data need somewhere to run. Inside Google, that somewhere was managed for years by a cluster manager called **Borg**.

The Borg paper describes a system that runs hundreds of thousands of jobs, from many thousands of different applications, across clusters that each hold up to tens of thousands of machines. It mixes latency-sensitive work (think search) with batch work (think analytics), squeezing every bit of utilization by packing tasks, over-committing resources, and letting low-priority jobs soak up whatever is left.

You may never have heard of Borg. But you have almost certainly heard of its descendant. Many of the concepts in **Kubernetes** trace directly back to it. When you write a deployment manifest today, you are using ideas that were forged inside a Google data centre long before they had a public name.

This is the pattern, over and over:

```text
Problem breaks every existing tool
        -> Google builds its own
        -> publishes the paper
        -> the industry spends a decade building open-source versions
```

## So... why does Google build everything itself?

It is tempting to read all this as arrogance. It is not. It is arithmetic.

Every solution in this article was forced by a number:

| Number | What it forced |
|---|---|
| 2 billion lines of code | A custom version-control service and a custom workspace filesystem |
| Tens of thousands of engineers committing daily | Custom build, test and review infrastructure |
| Billions of queries per second, globally | A custom database that fixes time itself |
| Hundreds of thousands of jobs across huge clusters | A custom cluster manager |

When your scale is large enough, the assumptions inside ordinary tools stop being true. A database that was perfectly designed for a million users has a quiet little ceiling baked into it somewhere. A version-control system built for a team of two hundred assumes you can copy everything to your laptop. The ceiling was fine, until you hit it.

And here is the part that circles back to our opening. Notice that none of these breakthroughs were about writing a clever function. They were about *noticing which assumption was about to break, and designing around it before it did.* That is system design. That is the job.

## And yes, about the crores

Let us talk money, because you asked, and because it is the honest answer to "why does anyone pay this much?"

Levels.fyi publishes self-reported, verified compensation data. For Software Engineers at Google in Greater Bengaluru (as of May 2026), the **median total compensation by level** looked like this:

| Level | Typical stage | Median total compensation |
|---|---|---|
| L3 | Entry level | about ₹40.7 lakh |
| L4 | Mid level | about ₹75.1 lakh |
| L5 | Senior | about ₹1.16 crore |
| L6 | Staff | about ₹2.19 crore |
| L7 | Senior staff | about ₹3.24 crore |

The overall median across levels was about ₹66 lakh.

A few important honesty notes before anyone books a flight:

- This is **self-reported data**, and a median is not a promise. Real offers vary widely with experience, negotiation, team and timing.
- "Total compensation" includes **stock and bonus**, not just salary. At the staff level, stock is a very large share of the package, and its value moves with the share price.
- The crore-plus numbers belong to **L5 and above**. Those are not first-job packages. They are what the company pays people who have *already* proven they can handle the harder half of the job.

Now ask the obvious question: if AI is writing most of the new code, what exactly is being purchased at L5, L6 and L7?

Not typing speed. Not syntax memory. The things the company cannot easily automate:

- **Judgment under uncertainty:** which of three plausible designs will still be sane in five years?
- **Blast-radius thinking:** what breaks, for whom, if this change is wrong?
- **Cross-team leverage:** a decision that quietly saves thousands of engineers an hour each.
- **Taste:** knowing which complexity is worth paying for, and which is vanity.

In other words, you are paid for being the person who understands the *system*, not the person who produces the most lines.

## How to actually get good at this

You do not need Google's scale to practise Google-style thinking. You need Google-style curiosity. A path I would recommend:

1. **Read the foundational papers**, in this order: the Potvin and Levenberg paper on the monorepo (short, readable, packed with numbers), the Borg paper, then the Spanner paper. Read each one asking only: *what assumption broke, and what did they build instead?*
2. **Read "Software Engineering at Google."** It is about processes and culture and has very little code, which is the whole point.
3. **Build tiny versions.** A toy build system with caching. A key-value store with replication. A little consensus protocol. You will learn more from one broken afternoon than from ten blog posts.
4. **Practise explaining trade-offs out loud.** Interviews at this level are rarely "write this function." They are "here is a problem that has outgrown its tools; tell me what you would do and what it would cost."

### Five questions to sharpen your system-design muscles

| # | Question | Ideas to explore |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Design a version-control system for a 2-billion-line codebase that 25,000 engineers use every day. | Service-based storage, virtual workspaces, caching, trunk-based development |
| 2 | How would you safely rename a function used in 100,000 files across 5,000 teams? | Automated refactoring, per-owner review splitting, staged rollout, rollback |
| 3 | Why can't you just trust server clocks to order transactions? What could you do instead? | Clock drift, bounded uncertainty, logical clocks, commit-wait |
| 4 | Design a build system that stays fast as the codebase grows 100x. | Hermetic builds, dependency graphs, incremental builds, remote caching |
| 5 | What are the real costs of a monorepo, and when would you refuse to adopt one? | Tooling investment, ownership, access control, team size, build scaling |

## The takeaway

Go back to where we started: a 200 GB game launching without breaking the internet. A repository the size of a small data centre. A database that waits out the uncertainty of time itself.

None of these exist because someone wrote brilliant code. They exist because someone looked at a problem that was about to outgrow its tools, understood it *deeply*, and had the nerve to build the thing that did not exist yet.

The code is the easy part. It always was. And in a world where machines increasingly type it for us, the people who will matter most are the ones who understand what to build, why it will break, and what happens next.

Learn to see systems. The rest is typing.
